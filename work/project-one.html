Southwest Airlines – Line Check Solver

Redesigning how check pilots reserve, track, and certify Line Checks.

Role
UX Summer Intern, Ops User Experience
Timeline
Summer 2026
Tools
Figma

Overview

A refresh for a tool pilots called “long overdue.”

Line Check Solver (LCS) is the internal tool Southwest check pilots use to reserve Line Checks: trips where they fly with and evaluate another pilot. They use it to find a trip that works, reserve it, and afterwards certify each pilot or rate them substandard.

On the Ops User Experience team, I worked on the pilot side of a redesigned LCS. We tested the prototype with 7 check pilots and turned what we saw into design changes for the MVP.

Screens use sample data. Some details are simplified for confidentiality.

Line Check Solver: Pilot Experience Summer 2026

The problem

Reserving a Line Check is a race, and every wrong click costs the trip.

Line Check reservations open all at once, and pilots compete to grab a slot the moment one is released. In that window, the old flow worked against them:

  1. 01

    Too much setup

    Pilots rebuilt the same search every time, even though most start and end at their home base.

  2. 02

    Options that weren’t really options

    Results looked available but hid evaluator-training conflicts, so pilots had to double-check them in a separate system before committing.

  3. 03

    The wrong details up front

    Pilots choose by pay and length of day, but those details weren’t where they looked first.

How we tested

7 check pilots, one clickable prototype, 30 minutes each.

We wanted to know if the redesign was usable, understandable, and fast enough for pilots’ real reservation mindset, and whether they’d trust it.

  • Who

    7 check pilots, the people who reserve and run Line Checks.

  • Format

    1:1 remote sessions, 30 minutes each, with screen share and think-aloud.

  • Tasks

    Reserve a Line Check, review it, then certify, rate substandard, and use bulk actions.

  • Interview

    Semi-structured questions on confidence, efficiency, and where each device fits.

The flow

From the Reservations page to a confirmed Line Check.

What we learned

Three themes shaped every design decision.

  1. 01

    Build for time-critical decisions

    Pilots race for slots, so the flow should run on smart defaults and as few steps as possible.

  2. 02

    Only show viable solutions

    Every participant asked us to hide options with conflicts instead of making them verify each one elsewhere. Invalid options made the whole tool feel unreliable.

  3. 03

    Surface pay and day details

    Pilots compare trips by total trip-for-pay, duty day, report and release time, and ground time. Those belong first, in that order.

Pilots searched and compared on a laptop, but monitored and certified on a tablet or phone, so the post-trip actions had to work on small screens too.

Design decisions · Reserve

Start pilots where they almost always start.

  1. 01

    Default stations to the last search

    Most pilots start and end at their home base.

  2. 02

    Default times to 00:00 – 23:30, with time zones

    Pilots include all hours to get the most solutions, and wanted to know whether times were Herb (Dallas) time or local.

  3. 03

    Default Max Legs to the highest option

    More legs means more pay in a single day, so that’s what pilots picked.

Design decisions · Compare

Make the best trip obvious at a glance.

  1. 01

    Lead with Total TFP and add release time

    Pilots scan pay first, then want to know when the day ends. Average TFP was dropped because no one used it.

  2. 02

    Collapse every row by default

    Pilots compare top-level numbers before drilling in, so auto-expanding the first solution got in the way.

  3. 03

    Cut the duplicate flight time

    Block time already covered it, so details stay grouped with the TFP.

  4. 04

    Handle evaluator conflicts

    Ideally, exclude solutions where the evaluator is actively training. For the MVP, add an “Actively Training” tag next to “Evaluator.”

  5. 05

    Rename “Create Line Check” to “Reserve Line Check”

    Pilots felt picking parameters was the “create” step, so the final button read as unclear.

Design decisions · Confirm

Put the instructions where pilots need them.

Pilots found “Back to Reservations” easily, and the confirmation page worked as an ending. One pilot looked for the Line Check “key areas of focus” here, which until then only lived in the Help Center, so we added them to the confirmation page too.

Design decisions · Certify

Keep the easy action easy, and the serious one deliberate.

  1. 01

    Type “Substandard,” not the pilot’s name

    Every pilot liked the extra friction for such a serious action. Typing a name risked typos, so the input now takes the word “Substandard” in any capitalization.

  2. 02

    Stop asking pilots to complete deadheads

    Pilots liked that deadheads no longer needed an action. Since it’s a process change, a Help Center FAQ explains it.

  3. 03

    Confirm the result with a toast

    Pilots understood the colored certified and substandard states, but some expected one more step. A page-level toast makes it clear they’re done.

Final designs

The recommendations, applied.

Smart defaults

Last-used stations, all-day times, and max legs pre-filled.

Pay and day first

Total TFP, report and release time lead every solution.

Safer substandard

Type “Substandard” to confirm, in any capitalization.

Help in context

Key areas of focus on the confirmation page, not just the FAQ.

Outcome

“We’re on the right track.”

Pilots’ overall sentiment was strongly positive. Our recommendations went to the product leads to decide what made the MVP, and pilots’ bigger requests became the roadmap: resolving reservation conflicts in the flow, checking crew legalities automatically, cross-referencing their schedule, and “Recommended for You” solutions.

Reflection

Reflection

  1. 01

    Design for the moment of use

    Knowing that reservations are a race changed what “good” meant. Fewer fields and smart defaults mattered more than any visual polish.

  2. 02

    Remove choices instead of explaining them

    An option that looks available but isn’t costs more trust than no option at all. Every pilot asked us to hide conflicts, not label them.

  3. 03

    Friction can be a feature

    For a high-stakes action like rating a pilot substandard, pilots wanted the extra step. The job was making that step safe, not removing it.
