Yoka Tea

A new website for an up-and-coming Seattle-based boba cafe.

Role
Product Designer
Timeline
Jan – May 2025
Tools
Figma

Overview

Yoka’s flyers and merch had a clear look. Its website needed one too.

Yoka Tea is a boba and specialty coffee shop on 34th Ave in Seattle. Its smiley mascot, cream-and-black flyers, and drinks like the Ube Halo Halo already had a following on Instagram.

Through Web Impact UW, a student organization that designs websites for local organizations, four of us designed a new site for Yoka from January to May 2025. We met with the owners regularly to show progress and get feedback.

My role

One of four designers on the team.

  • I owned

    The menu and gallery explorations, across mobile and desktop, from lo-fi to hi-fi.

  • I partnered on

    The moodboard, site map, style guide, and initial layouts.

Starting from the brand

We built the look from what Yoka already used.

Our moodboard pulled from Yoka’s flyers, merch, menu boards, and Instagram. The same things showed up again and again: the smiley mascot, cream and black, and typewriter-style text. Those became the style guide.

Structure

Menu, story, gallery, account, and cart.

We kept the top level of the site map short: the menu, the shop’s story, customer photos and reviews, an account, and a cart.

Exploring layouts

I sketched three menus and three galleries.

The menu and gallery hold most of the site’s content, so I tried very different layouts for each. We compared them as a team and walked the client through the options in our check-ins.

From lo-fi to hi-fi

The homepage at lo-fi, mid-fi, and hi-fi.

The order of the page stayed the same from the first sketch: a featured drink, the shop’s story, the signature drinks, and a newsletter sign-up. Each round swapped gray boxes for Yoka’s photos, colors, and type.

Final design

Four decisions in the final design.

  1. 01

    Show the drinks first

    The homepage opens on the Halo Halo Boba, then shows the five signature drinks with photos and short descriptions.

  2. 02

    Lay out the menu like the one in the shop

    I dropped the card grid and set the menu like Yoka’s printed menu board: coffee, matcha, signature drinks, and add-ons, with prices and a PDF download.

  3. 03

    Pair customer photos with reviews

    The gallery puts customers’ photos next to what they wrote, so visitors see real people at the shop.

  4. 04

    Use the brand on every page

    Smiley faces fill the background of the login and sign-up pages, and every page ends with the same black footer and newsletter sign-up.

Mobile

Every page, designed for mobile too.

I designed the menu and gallery for mobile alongside desktop, so neither needed a separate pass at the end. We also marked the spacing on each mobile screen for handoff.

Handoff

We presented the final designs to Yoka in May.

The handoff included desktop and mobile designs for every page, login and error states, and a spacing guide for the mobile screens.

Reflection

Reflection

  1. 01

    Bring options to the client

    Showing three menus and three galleries made our check-ins easier. The owners could point at what they liked instead of describing it from scratch.

  2. 02

    Start from the brand you already have

    Most of the site’s look came from things Yoka already made: the mascot, the flyers, the merch. We didn’t need to invent a new style.

  3. 03

    Design mobile at the same time

    Working on both screen sizes from lo-fi on meant the menu and gallery already worked on a phone when we reached hi-fi.
